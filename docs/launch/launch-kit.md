## Issue 1 — That delivery text can wait

**Subject:** A delivery text arrives. What should you check first?

**Preview:** Open the delivery service yourself before following a message.

You are waiting for a parcel. A text says there is a small redelivery fee. The amount looks harmless, and you are busy. That is a good moment to slow down.

Here is an invented example: “Your parcel could not be delivered. Update your address and pay the redelivery fee today.” It is an example of a pattern, not evidence about a particular delivery company.

Start somewhere you already trust: the retailer’s order confirmation, the delivery company’s app, or its website opened independently. Check whether the tracking information matches. A familiar sender name, logo or message thread does not settle who sent the message.

If you are still unsure, use [Ask Arthur’s free checker](https://askarthur.au/) for another assessment. Remove personal details first. A check can help you decide what to do; it cannot guarantee a message is safe.

**One action this week:** show someone in your household how to open their delivery tracking independently. That small habit is useful long after this particular message is gone.

Editorial source: [Scamwatch: delivery-message scams](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/about-us/news-and-alerts/expecting-a-delivery-during-the-holiday-sales-stop-and-think-don%E2%80%99t-click-the-link). This source is older guidance; do not reuse its historical figures as current statistics.

## Issue 2 — A familiar logo is only the beginning

**Subject:** Before you buy from that lookalike shop

**Preview:** Check the address, the seller and the payment request.

You find an advertisement for something you wanted. The shop looks polished. The logo is familiar. The sale is about to end.

Our invented example is a shop that copies a retailer’s colours and product photographs but uses a different web address. Presentation alone does not tell you who will receive your payment.

Pause before checkout. Reach the retailer through a route you have used before. Compare the address carefully. Look for information about the business outside its own website and advertisement. Treat pressure to pay quickly as a reason to investigate, particularly when the seller asks you to move to an unfamiliar payment arrangement.

A very low price is a prompt to check, not proof of a scam. Equally, a normal price and a padlock do not prove the seller is legitimate.

You can put the shop URL into [Ask Arthur’s free checker](https://askarthur.au/). Keep payment information and passwords out of your submission.

**One action this week:** save a trusted route to one retailer you use often, so your next purchase does not have to start from an advertisement.

Editorial source: [Scamwatch: buying and selling scams](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/types-of-scams/buying-and-selling-scams).

## Issue 3 — “Send me the code” deserves a pause

**Subject:** The code in your text message belongs with you

**Preview:** A friendly conversation can still contain an unsafe request.

An invented scenario: you are selling an item online. A buyer says they need to verify you and asks you to read out a code that has just arrived on your phone.

Before replying, read the whole message containing the code. Which service sent it? What action does it describe? Did you start that action yourself?

A stranger’s explanation should not replace those answers. Keep security codes private and use the platform’s own support route if you need help. Stop the conversation if the other person pressures you to act immediately.

The same pause is useful outside a marketplace. Someone claiming to help with an account problem can also ask you to do something that gives them access.

If you want help assessing the conversation, [check it with Ask Arthur](https://askarthur.au/) after removing the code and your personal information. Do not include the code in a screenshot submitted for analysis.

**One action this week:** agree with someone you trust that either of you can ask for a second opinion before responding to an urgent account request.

Editorial source: [Scamwatch: spot and avoid scams](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/stop-check-protect/help-to-spot-and-avoid-scams).

## Issue 4 — If you already replied, take the next step

**Subject:** Already interacted with a suspicious message?

**Preview:** What happened matters more than blaming yourself.

People encounter convincing scams while tired, distracted or under pressure. If you have already responded, focus on what the other person received and what needs attention now.

Did you only reply? Did you share a password? Did money leave an account? Those are different situations. Write down what happened while it is fresh, without continuing the conversation to gather more evidence.

If money or bank details are involved, contact your bank promptly through its app or a number you obtained independently. If an account password was exposed, open the real service yourself and follow its account-security process. Use official recovery and reporting routes rather than someone who contacts you promising to retrieve lost money for a fee.

Keep relevant messages and transaction references somewhere private. Avoid posting identity documents or complete financial details publicly.

Ask Arthur can help assess suspicious content, but you do not need to wait for a checker result before contacting your bank or account provider.

**One action this week:** save [Scamwatch’s Stop, Check, Protect guidance](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/stop-check-protect/in-your-language-scam-information/english) and share it with someone who would appreciate a calm starting point.

Editorial source: the linked Scamwatch guidance. Do not present this issue as personalised financial or account-recovery advice.

## Four-video experiment — after four newsletter issues

Use the same four topics as 60–120 second screen recordings with synthetic examples and readable captions. Structure: situation (10 seconds), independent check (45 seconds), limitation (10 seconds), one action and newsletter link (10 seconds). Never demonstrate on a live suspicious site, expose a real person’s messages or imply guaranteed detection.

Record with existing equipment; use no paid tools initially. Publish only after reviewing the examples, links, captions and account identity. Evaluate qualified newsletter signups and useful questions, not views alone. Continue only if the work fits within two hours per video and brings relevant readers; otherwise keep newsletter and partner distribution as the primary channels. These are proposed experiment limits, not audience forecasts.
